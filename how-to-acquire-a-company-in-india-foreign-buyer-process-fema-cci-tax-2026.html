How to Acquire a Company in India as a Foreign Buyer (2026): Process, Due Diligence, FEMA Pricing, CCI, Tax and Completion Filings, for foreign strategic buyers, PE funds and family offices.

A foreign buyer acquires an Indian company in six steps. Check the FDI route for the sector (most are automatic, some need government approval, and so do buyers with land-border beneficial owners). Run due diligence. Agree a price at no less than fair value backed by a valuation report. Get Competition Commission approval if the deal crosses the thresholds (for example a deal value above INR 2,000 crore where the target has substantial Indian operations). Sign with escrow or deferred payment of up to 25% for up to 18 months if needed. Complete and file Form FC-TRS within 60 days of the payment. A listed target adds a SEBI open offer at 25%. The Indian sellers pay capital gains tax. The buyer pays 0.015% stamp duty on the share transfer and should check whether the target's tax losses survive the change of ownership.

/ Due diligence: what usually comes up

Red flags we look for in Indian targets.

  • FEMA history: late or missing FC-GPR, FC-TRS, ODI or annual return filings; these need compounding before or at completion. See FEMA compounding.
  • Tax: TDS defaults, GST input credit mismatches, pending assessments and notices, transfer pricing on related-party dealings.
  • Company law: loans to directors, unfiled forms, share issues without proper valuation or approvals.
  • Revenue quality: customer concentration, cash sales, related-party revenue.
  • People and contracts: key employees, change-of-control clauses, leases and licences in the promoters' personal names.
  • See also transaction due diligence and due diligence for foreign buyers.

/ Tax and filings

Who pays what.

  • Sellers: taxed on capital gains (long-term at 12.5% on unlisted shares held over 24 months for residents); if a seller is non-resident, the buyer must withhold tax from the price.
  • Price below fair market value: income-tax deems fair market value for the seller and can tax the buyer on the shortfall, so price and valuation must line up.
  • Losses: a closely held company's carried-forward business losses generally lapse when more than 49% of voting shareholding changes hands.
  • Stamp duty: 0.015% of the consideration on a share transfer; asset deals attract state stamp duty on property and goods, but a business transferred as a going concern is exempt from GST.
  • FEMA: FC-TRS within 60 days of receipt or remittance of consideration; then FLA return annually.

FAQ

Common questions, answered.

Can a foreign company acquire an Indian company?
Yes. In most sectors a foreign buyer can acquire up to 100% under the automatic route; some sectors have caps or need government approval, and buyers with beneficial owners in land-border countries need approval.
What price must a foreign buyer pay for Indian shares?
No less than fair value, determined by a chartered accountant, SEBI-registered merchant banker or practising cost accountant using an internationally accepted method; listed shares follow SEBI pricing.
When is Competition Commission approval needed?
When the parties or target cross the asset or turnover thresholds and the de minimis exemption does not apply, or when the deal value exceeds INR 2,000 crore and the target has substantial business operations in India.
Can part of the price be paid later?
Yes. Up to 25% of the total consideration can be deferred or held in escrow for up to 18 months from the transfer agreement, within the pricing rules.
What filing is needed after an acquisition?
Form FC-TRS within 60 days of receipt or remittance of the consideration, plus ROC filings for board changes and the annual FLA return.
